## 📊 Step 2: Primary Statistical Test – Multivariable Survival Analysis (Time-to-Dementia)

### Objective  
Directly evaluate whether higher educational attainment is associated with reduced risk of developing dementia over time using a time-to-event model.

### Why this approach?
- Dementia is a **chronic progressive condition** with varying onset times.
- Standard cross-sectional regression fails to capture temporal dynamics.
- Survival models naturally handle censored data (subjects still cognitively intact at last follow-up).

### Methodology:
1. **Define Event Variable**
   - Event: `Cognitive status at UDS visit_Dementia == 1 OR Presumptive etiologic diagnosis of the cognitive disorder - Alzheimer's disease_Yes == 1`
   - Time: `Year_of_death` – `Subjects_age_at_visit` (if deceased), or `Year_of_death - Year_of_visit` (if known).  
     → Use **age-at-onset** as time-to-event if death occurs after symptom onset.

2. **Model Selection: Cox Proportional Hazards Regression**
   - Dependent variable: Time until dementia diagnosis/death (censoring when no event occurs).
   - Predictor: `Years_of_education`
   - Covariates to adjust for:
     - Age (`Subjects_age_at_visit`)
     - Sex (`Subject's sex_Female`, `Male`)
     - APOE genotype (use `Number of APOE e4 alleles_*` as dummy-coded predictors)
     - Race (as categorical: White, Black, Asian, etc.)
     - Hispanic/Latino status
     - Baseline cognitive scores (`MoCA_Total_Score_corrected_for_education`, `Total_MMSE_score_using_DLROW`) — include as continuous covariates

3. **Assumptions Check**
   - Verify proportional hazards assumption via Schoenfeld residuals.
   - If violated, consider stratified models or time-dependent covariates.

4. **Effect Size & Interpretation**
   - Report hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals.
   - Example interpretation: “Each additional year of education is associated with a 5% reduction in dementia hazard (HR = 0.95, 95% CI [0.91–0.99], p = 0.02).”

> ⚠️ Avoid simple linear regression — it ignores censoring and misrepresents risks in longitudinal populations.

## 🔬 Step 3: Robustness & Sensitivity Analyses

### Objective  
Test stability of results under different definitions, exclusions, and analytic choices.

### Proposed Analyses:
| Analysis | Purpose |
|--------|--------|
| **Subgroup Analysis** | Examine whether the effect differs by age group (<65, ≥65), sex, race, or APOE status (e.g., e4 carriers vs. non-carriers). Look for interactions. |
| **Logistic Regression (Cross-Sectional)** | Compare odds of dementia among individuals with >12 vs. ≤12 years of education, adjusting for key confounders. Use to validate findings from survival analysis. |
| **Mediation Analysis** | Test whether cognitive reserve manifests through improved performance on MoCA/MMSE — i.e., does education → better cognition → less dementia? Use Baron & Kenny or structural equation modeling (SEM). |
| **Sensitivity to Censoring** | Exclude subjects who died without dementia diagnosis and re-run survival analysis. Assess how much the HR changes. |

> 💡 These tests help rule out spurious associations and reveal whether effects are consistent across subpopulations.

## ⚠️ Critical Considerations & Caveats

- **Reverse Causality Risk**: Individuals with dementia might have had lower education earlier — though this is unlikely given the direction of the hypothesis, we must acknowledge it in discussion.
- **Confounding by Unmeasured Factors**: Socioeconomic status, income, occupational complexity, access to healthcare — these could mediate or distort the relationship. We cannot fully control them, but can adjust for proxies (e.g., MMSE score).
- **Education ≠ Cognitive Reserve**: High education may reflect general intelligence, not necessarily cognitive resilience. Clarify conceptual distinction in reporting.
- **Ethical Note**: Do not generalize findings to low-resource settings unless validated locally.
