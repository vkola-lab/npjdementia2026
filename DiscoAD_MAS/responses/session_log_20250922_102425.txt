### 🔬 Option 1: Linear Regression Model (Standard Association)
- **Approach**: Fit a linear regression model where processing speed (dependent variable) is predicted by hypercholesterolemia (independent variable), controlling for age and education level.
- **Why it works**: Directly tests the negative association; allows estimation of effect size and significance.
- **Refinements**:
  - Include interaction terms (e.g., hypercholesterolemia × age) to explore non-linear effects.
  - Use robust standard errors to account for potential outliers or heteroscedasticity.
- **Caveat**: Assumes linearity and normality of residuals; validate assumptions post-fit.

### 🔬 Option 2: Nonlinear Modeling (Polynomial or Spline-Based)
- **Approach**: Use polynomial regression (quadratic or cubic) or splines to test if the relationship between hypercholesterolemia and processing speed follows a curvilinear pattern (e.g., optimal mid-range cholesterol vs. decline at extremes).
- **Why it works**: Captures U-shaped or inverted U-shaped relationships that simple linear models may miss.
- **Validation**:

### 🔬 Option 3: Stratified Analysis by Age Group
- **Approach**: Split subjects into age groups (e.g., <60, 60–75, >75) and examine the strength and direction of the association within each group.
- **Why it works**: Tests whether the negative relationship varies across life stages—e.g., stronger in older adults due to cumulative neurodegeneration.
- **Insight Potential**: Identifies subgroups where hypercholesterolemia may have more pronounced effects.

### 🔬 Option 4: Mediation Analysis
- **Approach**: Test whether age or education mediates the relationship between hypercholesterolemia and processing speed.
- **Example Pathway**: Hypercholesterolemia → Age → Slower cognition (or education → better cognition → weaker impact of cholesterol).
- **Why it works**: Helps uncover underlying mechanisms behind observed associations.
- **Methodology**: Use structural equation modeling (SEM) or bootstrapped mediation analyses.

### 🔬 Option 5: Sensitivity Analysis & Subgroup Exclusion
- **Approach**: Remove outliers or extreme cases (e.g., very high cholesterol or extremely low MMSE/MoCA) and re-run key models to assess stability.
- **Why it works**: Ensures findings aren’t driven by anomalies.
- **Alternative Strategy**: Exclude participants with missing data on any critical variable to improve completeness.

### 🔬 Option 6: Time-Trend Analysis (if longitudinal data exists)
- **Approach**: If repeated measures exist over time, analyze changes in processing speed relative to evolving cholesterol levels.
- **Why it works**: Moves beyond cross-sectional correlation to infer causality or temporal dynamics.
- **Limitation**: Requires sufficient follow-up intervals; otherwise, use only baseline data.

## 🧪 Scientific Plan – Analytical Approaches to Compare Processing Speed Between Groups

You’re now investigating whether individuals classified as having hypercholesterolemia exhibit significantly lower cognitive processing speed compared to those who do not — using MMSE and/or MoCA scores as proxies.

### 🔍 Option 1: T-Test or Mann-Whitney U Test (Basic Group Comparison)
- **Approach**: Split participants into two groups based on a threshold for hypercholesterolemia (e.g., ≥180 mg/dL or ≥median value). Compare mean MMSE or MoCA scores between groups using an independent t-test (if normally distributed) or Mann-Whitney U test (non-parametric alternative).
- **Why it works**: Simple, interpretable, and directly answers the hypothesis.
- **Critical Considerations**:
  - Choose a clinically meaningful cutoff (e.g., based on literature, percentiles, or expert consensus).
  - Report effect sizes (e.g., Cohen’s d) alongside p-values to assess practical significance.
- **Sensitivity Check**: Repeat with different thresholds (e.g., 150, 170, 200 mg/dL) to see if results are stable.

### 🔍 Option 2: Logistic Regression with Categorical Outcome
- **Approach**: Treat “slower processing” as a dichotomous outcome (e.g., MMSE < 26 or MoCA ≤ 24 = slow; otherwise fast). Predict this outcome using hypercholesterolemia status as predictor.
- **Why it works**: Allows modeling probability of poor performance linked to cholesterol status.
- **Extension**: Add covariates (e.g., age, education) to adjust for confounders.
- **Output Focus**: Odds ratios (ORs) indicate how much more likely individuals with hypercholesterolemia are to have impaired processing.

### 🔍 Option 3: ANOVA / Kruskal-Wallis Test (Multi-Level Grouping)
- **Approach**: Instead of just two groups, categorize hypercholesterolemia into three levels: low (<150), moderate (150–199), high (≥200). Compare processing scores across these categories.
- **Why it works**: Reveals trends in how processing speed declines with increasing cholesterol levels — supports a graded effect.
- **Statistical Choice**:
  - Use one-way ANOVA (parametric) or Kruskal-Wallis (non-parametric) depending on distribution.
- **Post-hoc Testing**: Apply Bonferroni or Tukey correction if significant overall difference.

### 🔍 Option 4: Propensity Score Matching (PSM)
- **Approach**: Match individuals with hypercholesterolemia to those without, based on observable covariates (age, education, baseline health indicators), to reduce selection bias.
- **Why it works**: Mimics a randomized trial by balancing baseline characteristics between groups.
- **Steps**:
  - Estimate propensity scores using logistic regression.
  - Match pairs or create balanced cohorts.
  - Re-analyze processing speed differences in matched samples.
- **Benefit**: Increases validity of causal inference when unmeasured confounders are suspected.

### 🔍 Option 5: Stratified Analysis by Covariate (Age, Education)
- **Approach**: Break down comparisons by age group or education level to test whether the gap in processing speed widens or narrows under certain conditions.
- **Example**: Does the slowdown occur more strongly among older adults?
- **Why it works**: Uncovers subgroup-specific effects and improves generalizability.
- **Presentation Value**: Useful for policy or clinical recommendations targeting specific demographics.

### 🔍 Option 6: Bootstrapping Confidence Intervals
- **Approach**: Generate bootstrap samples (with replacement) to compute confidence intervals for the difference in means (MMSE/MoCA) between hypercholesterolemic and non-hypercholesterolemic groups.
- **Why it works**: Provides robust estimates even when sample size is small or distribution is skewed.
- **Advantage over traditional CI**: More reliable in real-world datasets with variability.
