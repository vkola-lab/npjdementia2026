### Option 2: Multivariate Regression Modeling

### Option 3: Propensity Score Matching (PSM) + Difference-in-Differences  
- **Approach**:

- **Key Considerations**:  
  - Define outcome as *change in cognitive status over time* (if longitudinal data exists).  
  - Evaluate balance after matching via standardized mean differences (<0.1 considered balanced).  
- **Strengths**: Reduces selection bias; mimics randomized trials.

- **Key Considerations**:  
  - Accounts for within-patient correlation.  
  - Can assess how cognitive status evolves over time relative to surgical intervention.  
- **Strengths**: Handles repeated measures well; captures individual trajectories.  
- **Limitations**: Requires sufficient number of repeated visits per subject; complex interpretation.

### Option 5: Stratified Analysis by Age Group

### Option 1: Comparative Cohort Analysis by Surgical Status  
- **Approach**: Compare baseline vs. post-surgery cognitive flexibility scores between patients who underwent heart valve replacement/repair and those who did not.  
- **Methodology**: Use a matched-pair design where pre- and post-operative cognitive assessments are compared within individuals (within-subjects). If paired observations are unavailable, use a two-group comparison (replaced vs. non-replaced) with adjustment for age, time since surgery, and visit date.  
- **Statistical Methods**: Paired t-test (if repeated measures exist), Wilcoxon signed-rank test (non-normal distributions), or mixed-effects models with random intercepts for subjects. Include covariates such as age, time from surgery, and month/year of assessment.  
- **Strengths**: Directly tests change over time; accounts for individual variability.  
- **Limitations**: Relies on sufficient pre/post data; may have missing timepoints due to follow-up gaps.  

### Option 2: Time-Series Modeling of Cognitive Change  
- **Approach**: Model cognitive flexibility as a function of time post-surgery using longitudinal trajectories.  
- **Methodology**: Fit growth curve models (e.g., linear or quadratic) to cognitive status over time, stratified by surgical intervention. Assess whether the slope of improvement differs significantly between groups.  
- **Statistical Methods**: Mixed-effects regression with time as a continuous variable, grouped by surgery status. Test interaction terms between surgery and time.  
- **Strengths**: Captures dynamic changes and potential delays in improvement.  
- **Limitations**: Requires dense temporal sampling; sensitive to missing data.  

### Option 3: Propensity Score Matching (PSM) for Causal Inference  
- **Approach**: Address selection bias by matching patients who received surgery with similar controls based on observable characteristics (age, comorbidities inferred via age and visit timing).  
- **Methodology**: Build propensity scores using logistic regression or machine learning (e.g., random forest) predicting likelihood of undergoing surgery given age, visit year/month, etc. Match cases and controls 1:1 or 1:2. Then compare cognitive outcomes before and after surgery.  
- **Statistical Methods**: Difference-in-differences (DiD) or conditional inference after matching. Perform sensitivity analysis for unmeasured confounding.  
- **Strengths**: More robust causal interpretation; handles selection bias.  
- **Limitations**: Assumes no unobserved confounders; requires careful validation of match quality.  

### Option 4: Stratified Analysis by Age Group  
- **Approach**: Examine whether cognitive flexibility improvement varies by patient age group (e.g., <60, 60–75, >75).  
- **Methodology**: Split the cohort into age strata and analyze change in cognitive status per stratum. Use ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis to assess differences across age groups in post-op improvement.  
- **Statistical Methods**: Multivariate regression with interaction term between age group and surgery status.  
- **Strengths**: Identifies subgroups most likely to benefit; clinically relevant.  
- **Limitations**: May reduce sample size per group; could miss nonlinear effects.  

### Option 5: Sensitivity Analysis Using Missing Data Imputation  
- **Approach**: Investigate how missingness affects results through imputation strategies.  
- **Methodology**: Apply multiple imputation (e.g., MICE) to handle missing cognitive status values or form dates. Re-analyze under different imputation assumptions (e.g., mean, median, predictive model).  
- **Statistical Methods**: Compare point estimates and confidence intervals across imputed datasets.  
- **Strengths**: Improves validity of results in real-world datasets with incomplete records.  
- **Limitations**: Adds complexity; imputation assumptions must be justified.
